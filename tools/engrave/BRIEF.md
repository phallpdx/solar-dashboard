# Master engraver brief: Morning Edition plates

**Client:** Parker, Sauvie Island, Portland OR. He reads this every morning on a Kindle Paperwhite 11.

**Verdicts so far:** "looks like shit", "reads like cheap wallpaper", "haphazard fucking bullshit".

**What he wants:**
- "Hire master engravers to custom engrave each page and fit the engraving to the contents of the page perfectly."
- "Extremely bespoke feeling."
- "Read like a hand bound book that costs millions."
- "Real art engraving, everywhere."

**Second reference:** tools/engrave/ref/hunt.jpg and ref/hunt-crop.jpg, a page of drawings by Grand Master gun engraver Ken C. Hunt. It shows:
- a wide border band of dense bas-relief acanthus, every leaf modelled, set on a shaded dark ground
- a free-standing acanthus cluster in the middle of the page
- lacy, Thai-like symmetrical pendants (top right)

Treat this as the vocabulary for leaf modelling and for the free-standing ornaments.

**Reference (his "this level"):** tools/engrave/ref/codex.jpg and ref/codex-crop.jpg, The Engraver's Codex by Sam Alfano. Study them. Its qualities:
- **Deep-relief acanthus scrollwork, fully shaded.** Every leaf has volume, made with fine parallel bulino and cross-hatch shading that runs dark into the curl and light on the turned edge. Outlines alone are not enough.
- **Big, confident, flowing scrolls.** They spiral into tight curls, overlap one another, and fill the space completely while framing a clear central cartouche or subject.
- **Figural elements carved into the scroll.** In the reference these are the bearded faces. For us: a dog's head, a heron, Mt. Hood, a drum, the St. Johns Bridge, a Thai kinnari, the sun.
- **Ribbon banners and architectural pieces:** a pedestal, knurled bars, beaded mouldings, a dark ground behind the scrolls (background cut away and stippled or line-shaded) so the relief pops.
- **One composition, never tiles.** No repeated stamps in a row, no sticker medallions, no wallpaper ground.

## KING TIER (Parker: "Recall I said this was for a king. I want this tier of engraving")
New references:
- tools/engrave/ref/king-vise.jpg: a GRS engraving ball vise, fully engraved
- tools/engrave/ref/king-colt.jpg: a presentation Colt with an inlaid portrait medallion
- tools/engrave/ref/king-hunt-scroll.jpg: Ken Hunt's dense scroll panels

These set the bar. What they share, and what our plates have lacked:
1. **Scale and density.** Fine scroll is SMALL and packed: dozens of tight, interlocking scrolls per panel, each with its own leaves. Our big chunky acanthus leaves read as clip art beside these. Drop the leaf size by 2–3× and multiply the count, so the scrollwork flows continuously over the whole field with no gaps.
2. **Matted background.** Between the scrolls, the ground is cut away and finely punched or stippled to a dark matte. Use fine black stipple or dense punch-dot. Never plain hatching or flat black.
3. **Every scroll is a true spiral.** Each one is a smooth logarithmic spiral ending in a tight curl, with leaves sprouting along it. Every leaf is shaded with fine parallel cuts on its shadow side and has a bright cut edge.
4. **Gold-line borders.** Fine double or triple border lines, beading and wire-inlay lines frame every panel and follow the shape of the piece.
5. **Inlaid medallion.** A portrait-style medallion, a scene in a ring of beading surrounded by scroll, as on the Colt cylinder. Use it for the page's subject.
6. **Flow.** Scrolls grow from a single stem source and branch out in continuous rhythm, like Hunt's panels. They are not placed pieces.

On e-ink at 618 px wide, use 0.5 px black lines for the fine cuts and at least 1 px for white lines in black. Use the -eink renders to find the finest density that still reads, then work at that density.

## The book itself (Parker sent an 18th-century Aeneid with its engraved plate)

## Tone and the burin (Parker sent Doré, Victorian wood engravings and Goltzius)
References in tools/engrave/ref/:
- tone-dore.jpg: Doré's Inferno
- tone-diana.jpg: "Diana at the Chase", a wood engraving
- tone-bewick-vignette.jpg: a shaped vignette of putti and vines
- tone-goltzius-bacchus.jpg: Bacchus after Goltzius

What they teach:
1. **Light emerging from dark.** The ground is rich and dark, made of dense layered lines. Never flat. Subjects glow out of it in the light, giving drama and depth.
2. **Swelling lines.** Tone and form come from lines that swell and taper as they follow the contour, like the muscle on the Bacchus, with cross-hatch layered over them in the shadows. This is the only legitimate use of hatching: it MODELS FORM and follows surfaces. Plain ruled lines are not allowed.
3. **Shaped plates.** Use cut corners, arched tops, ovals and lunettes instead of plain rectangles. The Bacchus oval has grotesque masks and foliage in the corners, and a caption ribbon at the foot.
4. **Abundance.** Vines, grapes, leaves and drapery fill every corner, and each element is modelled.

## No white (Parker's exact rule: "No white unless behind text")
A plate has no text behind it, so it has NO blank white areas at all. Cartouche interiors, the sky in a scene, the space between scrolls: everything is engraved, with cut-away grounds, sky hatching, water lines and stipple. Plain white survives only as the highlight on a leaf edge or a hatch gap. The art director measures this: any blank white region larger than about 12×12 px is a rejection.

## Grandmaster detail (Parker, again: "insane details. Grandmaster work.")
Spend your detail budget the way a master does.
- Every leaf gets its central vein, its secondary veins, serrated lobes and shading that follows the form.
- Every curl gets a tight spiral eye.
- Backgrounds are cut away and filled.
- Borders get beading.
- The figures carry fur, feather and wood-grain texture in bulino.
Parker: "I want things that look like they took an eternity." At reading distance it should look impossible that someone made this by hand this morning. If the plate looks "done" after one pass, it isn't.

## Personal, grown into the ornament (Parker: "This type shit but more personal")
The acanthus itself has to be about Parker. Do it the way the Codex grows bearded faces out of its scrolls: personal figures emerge from the leafwork and are carved in the same relief, not pasted on top. For example:
- a scroll that curls into his dog's head
- stems that turn into drumsticks
- leaves that sprout from a snare drum or a trumpet bell
- a scroll that becomes a heron's neck, or the St. Johns Bridge arches
- a Thai kranok flame that resolves into a scroll
Pick the ones that fit the page.

## Theme and Easter eggs (Parker asked for this twice, so it is mandatory)
- **The theme MUST match the page.** The vignette and every figural element come from what this page is about today. Read the page text, not just its title.
  - If the page lists the Blazers playing the Thunder, engrave a basketball and hoop.
  - If it is tides, engrave the tide and the coast.
  - If it is a record, engrave the record and its era.
  - If it is pollen, engrave the plant.
  A generic sun or leaf is a failure.
- **Every plate hides at least one Easter egg about Parker.** Make it small and engraved into the scrollwork so it rewards a close look:
  - his dog curled in a scroll
  - crossed drumsticks
  - a tiny ride cymbal
  - a jazz note or a bar of swing rhythm
  - a "P" monogram
  - the St. Johns Bridge
  - Mt. Hood
  - a heron from Sauvie Island
  - a Thai kranok flame or an elephant
  - a solar panel
  - a Blazers pinwheel
  Where you can, tie it to the page (a dog paw print in the garden soil, drumsticks crossed behind the music cartouche). Vary which egg you use so pages don't repeat each other. Mention it in your return.

## Your job
You own `solar/plates/<slug>.py` for your pages. Each file defines:

    def plate(w, h, ctx) -> str   # a complete SVG, width=w height=h viewBox="0 0 w h"

Every morning the build calls it with the exact free rectangle left on each sheet of the page (w is 520–570, h is 50–560). ctx holds title, kicker, section, sample (today's page text), slug, date and sheet. The plate must fill the WHOLE rectangle edge to edge at every h, and the composition must change with the aspect:
- h < 110: a carved band
- 110–260: a cartouche with flowing scrolls
- over 260: a full scene with a banner and pedestal

The page's content is in tools/engrave/page-items.json. Earlier art director notes for some pages are in tools/engrave/ad-feedback.json. Fix those first.

Shared house library: solar/engraving_codex.py. Import it with `import engraving_codex`; the build puts solar/ on sys.path. Don't edit the shared modules. Copy a helper into your plate file if you need to change it.

## Hard rules (Kindle e-ink; read tools/engrave/eink-guide.md §1–2)
- Only #000, #fff, and exact greys (#111 #222 … #eee). Tone comes from hatching, not grey fills, wherever possible.
- Strokes 0.5, 1 or 1.5 CSS px. Hatch pitch 1.5, 2 or 3. Never leave a clear gap under 1 px between anti-aliased lines.
- Each plate: ≤ 600 SVG elements (pack hatching into one `<path d>` per tone) and ≤ 200 KB.
- No `<text>` (any lettering must be drawn as paths), no masks, filters, foreignObject, or CSS transforms. A `transform=` attribute on SVG elements is fine.
- Pure Python standard library, deterministic for a given (w, h, ctx), and under 1 s per call.

## Your loop
1. Run `bash tools/engrave/try.sh <slug>`. It renders tools/engrave/out/<slug>/z-<w>x<h>.png at 8 heights, an -eink.png simulation of each, and a budget check.
2. LOOK at the PNGs with Read: the 90, 180, 320 and 548 heights at least. Judge them against the references as the grandmaster engraver (line work, modelling, detail, fit) and as the art director (theme fits the page, Easter egg, composition, no white, no repetition). Both must sign off.
3. Iterate until it is genuinely beautiful. Never leave a plate failing the budget check.
